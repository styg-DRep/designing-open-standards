## Core principle

**Match the method to the uncertainty.**

Do not assume that all standards should be developed through the same process.

The development approach should reflect:

- how well understood the opportunity is;
    
- how stable the requirements are;
    
- how many actors must participate;
    
- how expensive mistakes are;
    
- how quickly evidence can be obtained;
    
- how technically complex the standard is;
    
- how much legitimacy depends upon formal process;
    
- how much variation and experimentation remain useful.
    

---

# Development Method Spectrum

Rather than choosing only between “formal” and “agile,” treat method as a configurable spectrum.

## Exploratory

Best when:

- the problem or opportunity is still uncertain;
    
- use cases are emerging;
    
- substantial experimentation is needed;
    
- the correct standardization surface is unclear.
    

Typical practices:

- prototypes;
    
- discovery;
    
- experiments;
    
- short feedback cycles;
    
- temporary schemas;
    
- competing approaches.
    

Primary goal:

> Learn what should be standardized.

---

## Iterative

Best when:

- the opportunity is understood reasonably well;
    
- implementation details remain uncertain;
    
- users can test developing versions;
    
- rapid learning matters.
    

Typical practices:

- versioned drafts;
    
- short cycles;
    
- regular testing;
    
- backlog prioritization;
    
- frequent releases;
    
- issue-driven development.
    

Primary goal:

> Improve the standard through repeated encounters with use.

---

## Consensus-led

Best when:

- legitimacy depends heavily on broad agreement;
    
- multiple institutions must coordinate;
    
- incompatible adoption would be costly;
    
- contested concepts require deliberation.
    

Typical practices:

- working groups;
    
- structured deliberation;
    
- proposal and objection processes;
    
- public drafts;
    
- recorded decisions;
    
- explicit consensus or consent thresholds.
    

Primary goal:

> Build sufficiently legitimate shared agreement.

---

## Formal / Sequential

Best when:

- requirements are relatively stable;
    
- compliance matters;
    
- downstream implementation costs are high;
    
- a recognized standards body or institutional process is involved;
    
- formal approvals are required.
    

Typical practices:

- requirements specification;
    
- staged drafting;
    
- formal reviews;
    
- controlled ballots or approvals;
    
- defined release criteria.
    

Primary goal:

> Produce a highly controlled and dependable specification.

---

## Hybrid

Most Cardano governance standards are likely to combine these.

For example:

```text
discovery
   ↓
iterative prototypes
   ↓
community testing
   ↓
consensus on core
   ↓
formal release
```

Do not force the entire lifecycle into one method.

# Method Selection Pattern

Ask:

## Requirements

- How clear are the use cases?
    
- How stable are the requirements?
    
- How much remains unknown?
    

## Participation

- Who must meaningfully participate?
    
- Can they participate continuously?
    
- Is asynchronous contribution necessary?
    
- Are there groups whose legitimacy requires deliberate inclusion?
    

## Risk

- What happens if the standard is wrong?
    
- Can errors be reversed cheaply?
    
- Could an early release create path dependency?
    
- Could incompatible implementations fragment governance infrastructure?
    

## Learning

- Can assumptions be tested quickly?
    
- Are early adopters available?
    
- Can prototypes reveal important unknowns?
    

## Legitimacy

- Does the standard require broad ecosystem recognition?
    
- Does any formal institution need to recognize it?
    
- Will adoption depend more on utility, consensus, mandate, or some combination?
    

## Resources

- What method can the current community realistically sustain?
    

---

# Pattern: Use the Least Heavy Process That Protects What Matters

Formal process is not inherently more legitimate.

Lightweight process is not inherently more open.

Choose the minimum procedural weight required to protect:

- quality;
    
- participation;
    
- transparency;
    
- interoperability;
    
- legitimacy;
    
- reversibility;
    
- accountability.
