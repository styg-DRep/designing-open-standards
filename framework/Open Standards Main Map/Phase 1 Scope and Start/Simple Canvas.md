# Simple Canvas

## Intent

Create a shared, revisable first view of a proposed open governance standard before substantial development begins.

The Simple Canvas helps participants make their current assumptions visible: **why change may be needed, who is implicated in it, what kind of shared asset might help, and what conditions could enable or prevent its development and adoption.**

It is intended to make an emerging standards effort easier to inspect, discuss and improve—not to establish that the proposed standard is necessary or that its proposed form is correct.

## When to use it

Use the Simple Canvas when an individual or group is:

- considering whether a recurring governance problem may benefit from a reusable agreement;
    
- beginning an effort to develop an open governance standard;
    
- trying to communicate an early standards concept to potential participants;
    
- exploring an existing standards effort whose assumptions have not yet been made explicit; or
    
- returning to an effort whose purpose, participants or environment may have changed.
    

The canvas can be entered with substantial uncertainty. Unknowns, disagreements and placeholders are useful outputs because they expose work that still needs to be done.

## What it is

The Simple Canvas is a **single-view orientation and framing tool**.

It captures the major assumptions presently supporting an open-standard effort:

- the opportunity being addressed;
    
- the kind of standard or agreement being considered;
    
- the asset the effort proposes to make available;
    
- anticipated uses;
    
- affected and participating people or groups;
    
- likely early adopters;
    
- possible engagement pathways;
    
- expected impacts;
    
- major risks; and
    
- resources already available or likely to be required.
    

Its primary value is not completeness. Its value is making an emerging model of the effort **visible enough to question**.

## What it is not

The Simple Canvas is not:

- **a detailed specification** — it should expose assumptions and gaps rather than resolve every one of them;
    
- **a roadmap** — it can inform subsequent planning, but does not prescribe the development path;
    
- **evidence that a new standard is needed** — existing standards, processes or institutions may already address the opportunity;
    
- **a governance mandate** — completing a canvas does not grant authority to speak for affected communities or proceed on their behalf;
    
- **a complete development guide** — later nodes deepen, test and potentially overturn claims made here.
    

## Principle

**Start with a shared hypothesis, not a predetermined solution.**

The canvas records the best current understanding of an opportunity and a possible response to it. Every part of that understanding remains open to correction through research, participation, testing and experience.

A healthy canvas therefore contains not only answers but visible uncertainty.

# START

## 1. Vision

Express the possibility motivating the effort as a short **“What if…?”** question.

The vision should describe a desirable change in conditions without prematurely specifying the mechanism that will produce it.

Ask:

- What becomes easier, clearer, safer or more possible?
    
- For whom?
    
- What present difficulty would become less constraining?
    
- Can the vision be understood without already accepting the proposed standard?
    

**Example**

_What if changes to Cardano governance could be shared, compared and discussed in a transparent and accessible way that accelerated effective improvement?_

`vision-statement`

## 2. High-level details

Give the effort enough identity that people can refer to it.

Record:

- a working name for the effort;
    
- the people or groups currently initiating it;
    
- known stewards or conveners, if any.
    

Placeholders are acceptable.

Avoid presenting an initiating group as the owner of a commons-oriented standard simply because it began the work.

### Produces

`effort-identity`

`initial-stewardship-context`

# WHY

## 3. The Standard

Describe **what kind of reusable agreement might be needed**.

Ask:

- What would people need to agree upon?
    
- Is the likely output a shared vocabulary, schema, protocol, specification, model, register, template, guide, process, or combination?
    
- What needs to become consistent, repeatable, comparable or mutually understandable?
    

Keep this description concise and aligned with the vision.

At this stage, the form is provisional. Subsequent work may show that a different kind of standard—or no new standard—is appropriate.

### Produces

`standard-hypothesis`

---

## 4. The Opportunity

Describe the condition that makes this effort worth exploring.

This replaces a narrow understanding of a “problem statement” with an **opportunity frame**: a concrete condition in the governance ecosystem where coordination, understanding or action could plausibly improve.

Ask:

- What is difficult now?
    
- Who encounters that difficulty?
    
- What becomes possible if that condition changes?
    
- What evidence do we currently have?
    
- What are we merely assuming?
    

The opportunity should remain intelligible independently of the proposed asset.

**Bad framing**

> Governance proposals need our new template.

**Stronger framing**

> Participants currently struggle to compare materially different governance proposals because important claims, impacts and assumptions are expressed inconsistently.

### Produces

`opportunity-frame`

`initial-assumptions`

`initial-evidence-needs`

---

## 5. The Asset

Describe what the effort might make available so that others can respond to the opportunity.

Think of this as more than “the solution.”

An open governance standard should increase the ability of ecosystem participants to coordinate and create solutions rather than concentrating solution-making authority in the standards group itself.

Ask:

- What reusable capability would the standard create?
    
- What could others do with it that is difficult now?
    
- Does it expand the agency of participants?
    
- How does it compare with assets that already exist?
    

The proposed asset remains a hypothesis until tested against the ecosystem.

### Produces

`asset-hypothesis`

---

## 6. Use Cases

Make the proposed relationship between **vision → opportunity → standard → asset** concrete.

Describe situations in which identifiable actors use the standard or its associated assets to accomplish something.

Good use cases identify:

- an actor;
    
- a context or need;
    
- an interaction with the standard;
    
- an outcome.
    

Generate broadly at first, then identify a few use cases that best expose whether the standard could actually create value.

Use cases are hypotheses to test, not promises about future adoption.

### Produces

`initial-use-cases`

---

# WHO

## 7. Key Participants

Identify the people and groups whose participation matters to the legitimacy, usefulness, development, maintenance or use of the standard.

Look beyond organisations formally associated with governance.

Include, where relevant:

- people experiencing the opportunity or problem;
    
- intended users;
    
- people whose behavior would need to change;
    
- maintainers and stewards;
    
- implementers;
    
- technical or domain specialists;
    
- funders and resource providers;
    
- existing institutions;
    
- people indirectly affected by adoption;
    
- people currently unable or unwilling to participate.
    

Distinguish **being affected** from **having authority**, and distinguish **participating in development** from **representing a community**.

Do not treat a stakeholder list as evidence that those stakeholders have actually been engaged.

### Produces

`initial-participant-map`

`participation-gaps`

---

## 8. Early Adopters

Identify specific people or groups capable of encountering the standard in practice early enough to influence its development.

Early adopters are not merely supporters.

Their value is that they can expose whether the proposed agreement works under real conditions.

Prefer specific names or identifiable groups when possible.

Ask:

- Who has the relevant need now?
    
- Who can realistically experiment with an early version?
    
- Who is willing to provide meaningful feedback?
    
- Are the likely early adopters sufficiently varied to expose different conditions?
    

### Produces

`early-adopter-hypothesis`

---

## 9. Engagement

Identify the places, channels and practices through which the effort can remain in contact with its participants and its wider environment.

This is not merely a communications plan.

Engagement should enable information to **flow back into the development effort**.

Consider:

- existing community spaces;
    
- forums and repositories;
    
- working groups;
    
- workshops;
    
- social channels;
    
- governance bodies;
    
- public calls;
    
- asynchronous participation;
    
- mechanisms for collecting structured feedback;
    
- pathways for people at the edges of existing governance communities.
    

Ask not only **“Where can we tell people?”** but **“Where can the effort listen?”**

### Produces

`engagement-hypothesis`

`feedback-channels`

---

# HOW

## 10. Key Impacts

Describe what could meaningfully change if the standard is developed, adopted and used.

Consider impacts on different participants separately.

Include intended benefits, but avoid treating them as established outcomes.

Ask:

- What becomes possible?
    
- What becomes easier?
    
- What becomes constrained?
    
- Who benefits?
    
- Who bears additional costs?
    
- What existing relationships or practices might change?
    

These statements form initial hypotheses that later work can turn into success criteria and evidence.

### Produces

`impact-hypotheses`

---

## 11. Major Risks

Identify conditions that could derail development, undermine legitimacy, prevent adoption or create harm through successful adoption.

Consider risks including:

- technical;
    
- governance;
    
- social;
    
- political;
    
- legal;
    
- economic;
    
- accessibility;
    
- privacy;
    
- temporal;
    
- maintenance;
    
- capture;
    
- representation;
    
- interoperability.
    

Where possible, pair the risk with an initial mitigation or question to investigate.

Pay particular attention to **success risks**: harms that could arise if the standard works exactly as intended.

### Produces

`risk-hypotheses`

`initial-mitigations`

`open-risk-questions`

---

## 12. Key Resources

Identify capabilities and resources the effort can draw upon or may need to acquire.

Resources may include:

- funding;
    
- contributor time;
    
- facilitation;
    
- domain expertise;
    
- technical infrastructure;
    
- existing standards;
    
- repositories and tools;
    
- governance interfaces;
    
- research;
    
- communication channels;
    
- institutional relationships;
    
- legal or licensing support;
    
- communities willing to test or implement the work.
    

Do not limit this section to resources controlled by the initiating group. Open standards develop within an ecosystem of existing capabilities and relationships.

`resource-map`

# Completion condition

The Simple Canvas is complete enough to move forward when it creates a **legible shared hypothesis of the effort**, including visible uncertainty.

Completeness does not require every field to have a confident answer.

A useful canvas should make it possible for another participant to ask:

1. What change are we trying to enable?
    
2. What condition makes that change worth exploring?
    
3. Why might a standard be useful here?
    
4. What might people actually use?
    
5. Who needs to be involved?
    
6. What assumptions are we making?
    
7. What could undermine or be harmed by this effort?
    
8. What do we already have to work with?
    
9. What do we still need to learn?
    

If these questions cannot yet be answered—or if participants disagree about their answers—the disagreement becomes an explicit input to scoping.

# What happens next?

Do not treat the completed canvas as authorization to proceed directly into development.

Use it to:

- share the emerging effort publicly;
    
- invite critique from affected people and potential adopters;
    
- identify gaps that require research;
    
- investigate existing standards and adjacent efforts;
    
- deepen the ecosystem and participant map;
    
- test whether the opportunity has been framed appropriately;
    
- determine whether creating a new standard is justified.
    

The canvas should remain available throughout the standards lifecycle.

Return to it when evidence changes the understanding of the opportunity, participants, impacts, risks, resources or proposed asset.

A substantial mismatch between the current effort and the canvas is not necessarily a failure. It is a signal that the effort's underlying model has changed and should be made explicit.
