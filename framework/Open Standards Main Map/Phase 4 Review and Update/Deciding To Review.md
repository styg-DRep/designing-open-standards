## Intent

Determine whether a formal review should begin, why it is needed, and what should be examined.

A review may begin because of:

- time;
    
- evidence;
    
- adoption;
    
- defects;
    
- ecosystem change;
    
- governance pressure;
    
- sustainability concerns;
    
- policy or regulation;
    
- requests for change;
    
- loss of relevance.
    

---

# Review Trigger Types

## Scheduled

A previously agreed review date or interval has been reached.

## Adoption

The standard has reached a level of use where wider consequences can now be evaluated.

## Evidence

Enough operational evidence exists to assess effectiveness.

## Defect

Errors or interoperability problems have accumulated.

## Change Request

A meaningful volume or class of requests requires reconsideration of the current standard.

## Ecosystem Change

Relevant infrastructure, policy, institutions, technologies, or standards have changed.

## Sustainability

Maintenance, governance, funding, or support is becoming fragile.

## Harm / Risk

Evidence suggests unintended consequences or ecosystem harm.

## Scope Drift

Actual use has moved materially beyond the originally justified scope.

---

# Pattern: Review Triggers Should Be Public

The governance model for the standard should explain:

- what can trigger review;
    
- who can invoke it;
    
- whether any threshold applies;
    
- who decides that review begins;
    
- what happens in urgent cases.
    

This allows review to be predictable rather than discretionary.

---

# Review Scope

Not every review needs to reconsider everything.

Define the scope explicitly.

Possible scopes include:

- full lifecycle review;
    
- adoption review;
    
- technical review;
    
- interoperability review;
    
- governance review;
    
- sustainability review;
    
- impact review;
    
- accessibility review;
    
- narrow feature review.
    

Ask:

> What evidence triggered the review, and what questions does that evidence require us to answer?
