## Intent

Make explicit the causal reasoning connecting the standard to the change it hopes to support.

This is deliberately a **minimal Theory of Change pattern**, not an endorsement of a specific ToC methodology.

Its purpose is to expose enough causal logic to improve the aims and objectives.

---

# Minimal Theory of Change Pattern

Start with six questions.

## 1. What pattern or condition exists now?

Use the work already completed in **Frame the Opportunity** and **Understand the Ecosystem**.

Ask:

- What behavior is recurring?
    
- What relationships sustain it?
    
- What conditions shape it?
    
- Where is tension visible?
    
- What currently prevents a better state?
    

---

## 2. What pattern do we want instead?

Describe the desired change in the behavior or capabilities of the ecosystem.

Avoid describing only the artifact.

Not:

> Everyone uses the standard.

But:

> Participants can exchange governance information without repeatedly renegotiating its meaning and structure.

---

## 3. What would need to change for that pattern to emerge?

Look for changes in:

- knowledge;
    
- capabilities;
    
- behavior;
    
- relationships;
    
- infrastructure;
    
- incentives;
    
- expectations;
    
- access;
    
- conventions.
    

These become candidate **outcomes**.

---

## 4. How could the standard contribute?

Identify the mechanisms connecting the standard to those outcomes.

For example:

```text
shared vocabulary
      ↓
less semantic ambiguity
      ↓
more comparable governance records
      ↓
easier cross-tool interpretation
```

The attached NPC guide calls particular attention to this “missing middle”: connecting activities to outcomes and impact rather than simply asserting that the intervention will cause the desired result.

It also treats **change mechanisms** as especially important: the mechanism describes how engagement with an intervention is expected to make an outcome more likely.

---

## 5. What conditions must support the change?

Ask:

- What must other actors do?
    
- What infrastructure must exist?
    
- What incentives must align?
    
- What cannot be controlled by the standards effort?
    
- What ecosystem conditions could help or hinder adoption?
    

The NPC approach similarly returns to external stakeholders and enabling factors after developing the internal change pathway, asking what others must do and what outside conditions can help or hinder success.

---

## 6. Where could our reasoning be wrong?

Identify:

- assumptions;
    
- weak causal links;
    
- contested beliefs;
    
- missing actors;
    
- unintended consequences;
    
- likely failure modes.
    

Ask:

> What would our strongest critic challenge?

This is a particularly useful idea from the attached ToC guide: assumptions are treated as the weak, uncertain, or untested parts of the causal theory and therefore become priority learning questions.

---

# Minimal ToC output

The lightweight output can be represented as:

```text
CURRENT PATTERN
      ↓
STANDARD INTERVENTION
      ↓
CHANGE MECHANISM
      ↓
NEARER-TERM OUTCOME
      ↓
ECOSYSTEM PATTERN CHANGE
      ↓
INTENDED IMPACT
```

surrounded by:

```text
conditions
assumptions
actors
risks
feedback
```

Do not force the pathway to be strictly linear.

Governance ecosystems contain feedback, parallel pathways, and emergent effects.

The chain exists to expose reasoning, not claim deterministic causality.

---

# Connection to the Four-Principles Change Explorer

The more detailed change explorer supplied with this effort provides a useful systems interpretation underneath this lightweight version:

**observe current patterns → notice tensions → understand the conditions shaping those patterns → identify possible actions → act → observe what changes → iterate.**

It also emphasizes that ecosystem-level patterns emerge from local choices and interactions, while system conditions simultaneously shape those local actions.

The standards-specific implication is important:

> A standard does not directly “change the ecosystem.”

It changes **conditions, information, affordances, expectations, or constraints** under which ecosystem actors make decisions.

Those actors then change—or do not change—the patterns of the whole.

The lightweight ToC should preserve that insight without requiring participants to work through the complete explorer.

---

# Pattern: Escalate When the Causal Logic Is Hard

The minimal ToC is sufficient when the causal pathway is reasonably clear.

Use a dedicated Theory of Change process when:

- participants disagree strongly about how change will occur;
    
- the standard targets systemic or behavioral change;
    
- important causal links are uncertain;
    
- many actors must change behavior;
    
- adoption is struggling;
    
- an existing standard is failing to create its intended impact;
    
- review reveals substantial unintended consequences.
    

The [NPC model](https://www.thinknpc.org/resource-hub/theory-of-change-for-funders-planning-to-make-a-difference/) provides one possible structured path: situation analysis → target groups → impact → outcomes → activities → mechanisms → sequencing → diagram → enabling factors → assumptions.

Its useful lesson is not that standards work should adopt those exact ten steps, but that difficult causal reasoning can be decomposed and investigated systematically.

A developed ToC should remain a **living model** that is revisited as evidence accumulates, rather than a one-time planning artifact.

This creates a future relationship:

**Review —returns-to→ Theory of Change**

when observed outcomes diverge from expected ones.
